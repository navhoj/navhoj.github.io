Rent Caps, Spillovers, and Investment: A Dynamic Housing Game with Landlord Portfolios
Working Paper

Rent regulation protects incumbents, but where do the welfare losses come from; muted investment, tenant misallocation, mobility lock-in, markup distortion, or cross-border spillovers? Existing work documents that losses exist but cannot decompose them, because the available frameworks are either static, single-jurisdiction, or lack a planner benchmark on the same state space. I develop a dynamic game in which landlords with cross-jurisdiction portfolios set new-lease rents against downward-sloping residual demand, maintain a durable quality stock, and face incumbent rent-increase caps, eviction costs, and permit delays. The model generates three empirically documented patterns from a single mechanism markup shifting under binding caps: incumbent-new rent wedges, cross-border spillovers, and renovictions. Having all three in one framework makes welfare decomposition possible. I implement a Shapley-based attribution that evaluates neutralised environments on identical state-action supports, yielding an exact additive decomposition of the planner decentralised gap into five named channels. Calibrated to five moments from San Francisco, Berlin, New York, Cambridge~MA, and Scotland, the model implies that mobility and investment account for roughly 60\% of the welfare gap; but cross-border spillovers — which receive almost no policy attention — account for a larger share of the welfare gap than the incumbent–new rent wedge in every specification that matches the calibration targets. The decomposition method is portable to any regulated durable-goods setting where counterfactuals can be expressed as parameter substitutions.

Key Words: rent regulation; spatial spillovers; landlord portfolios; maintenance investment; Markov-perfect equilibrium; welfare decomposition

Freeze at the Bottom, Heat at the Top: Distributional and Cross-Border Impacts of Scotland's Rent Cap

Working Paper: 2024

In October 2022 Scotland imposed a 0% cap on in tenancy rent increases, relaxed to 3% in April 2023 and lifted in March 2024. We study how this temporary intervention reshaped the distribution of advertised rents for new lettings within Scotland and whether demand spilled across the border into England. Using approximately 900,000 geocoded Rightmove listings (2019 to 2024), we embed difference in differences in a spatial quantile regression framework that allows treatment effects and spatial dependence to vary across the rent distribution. Within Scotland, exploiting cross council variation in pre policy private rented sector intensity as a continuous dose, we find that the cap compressed lower tail listing rents by about 4% at the 25th conditional percentile while raising upper tail rents by roughly 6% at the 75th percentile, a pattern consistent with binding constraints protecting the lower segment and selection, quality upgrading, and compositional shifts elevating the upper segment. Spatial diagnostics reveal quantile specific structure: spatially correlated unobservables at the lower tail and direct price propagation across neighbouring markets at the upper tail. Along the Scotland and England border, a synthetic control comparison shows that adjacent English authorities experienced a persistent 2 to 3% rent premium relative to well matched inland controls, with suggestive statistical significance. Phase decomposition indicates that lower tail protection was strongest during the strict freeze, while upper tail pressures intensified under the relaxed cap. The findings demonstrate that temporary in tenancy rent caps can deliver near term relief at the bottom of the new lettings distribution but may simultaneously generate upward pressure at the top and modest cross border displacement, an equity and efficiency tension with direct implications for the design of emergency rent regulation.

Key Words: Rent Control; Hedonic Pricing; Spatial Quantile Regression; Difference in Differences; Cross Border Spillovers; Scotland

Herding Behavior and Government Policy Responses: Evidence from COVID-19 Effect
(joint with M Nouri-Goushki)
2023; Heliyon

This study investigates the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on investor behavior in Iran's stock market, focusing on herding behavior. Using data from the top 50 companies over 2,381 days and government response data from the Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker, the findings reveal that the pandemic intensified herding behavior, exacerbated by market volatility. Moreover, the government's response failed to reduce this behavior. These results highlight the potential for herding to distort market prices and emphasize the need for policymakers and investors to address such behavioral biases to prevent misvaluations.

Key Words: Machine Learning; Behavioural Economics; Behavioural Finance; Systematic Literature Review.

Machine Learning in Behavioural Finance: A Systematic Literature Review
(joint with M yahyazadehfar and B Abedin)
Summer of 2022;The Journal Of Financial Data Science

This study endeavors to investigate the application of machine learning in behavioural economics and behavioural finance to represent a profile of studies conducted in this field. To accomplish this task, 90 scientific studies have been systematically extracted between 2000 and June 1, 2020. Utilizing the text analysis techniques and related statistical methods, the abstracts of the extracted studies have been reviewed and analyzed. First, it was found that attention to this field has developed in recent years with an accelerating trend. Second, it has been demonstrated that specialized journals have also bestowed more curiosity in these studies than in the past by publishing more relevant studies. Third, results revealed that machine learning has been applied in areas such as investor sentiment, decision making, consumer behaviour, trading strategies, game theory, and other areas in the field of behavioural economics and behavioural finance. In this regard, the application of machine learning had been included techniques such as Support Vector Machine, Regression, Neural Networks, Random Forest, and so on. Despite the expanding consideration adjusted to this field by researchers and specialized journals, there are still many research gaps in this field. Accordingly, there is a relatively significant distance until fully unleashing the superior powers of machine learning, like prediction and classification in behavioural economics and behavioural finance. Finally, this research has completed its mission by suggesting implications for the future of this field based on the acquired outcomes.

Key Words: Machine Learning; Behavioural Economics; Behavioural Finance; Systematic Literature Review.
